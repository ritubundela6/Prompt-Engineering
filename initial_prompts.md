## 1. Blog post: Why remote work is good

### User prompt

> Blog Post: Write a blog post about why remote work is good.

### Assistant response

# Why Remote Work Is Good for Employees and Businesses

Remote work has changed where many people do their jobs. Instead of spending every workday in an office, employees can work from home or another suitable location. When teams have the right tools and clear expectations, that flexibility can benefit both employees and businesses.

One of the biggest advantages is time. A commute can take a substantial part of the day, leaving less room for family, exercise, hobbies, or rest. Working remotely gives people some of that time back. It can also make everyday responsibilities easier to manage without taking attention away from the job.

Remote work can help people focus, too. An office is useful for quick conversations and teamwork, but it can also bring interruptions. At home, employees may have more control over their workspace and can set aside uninterrupted time for tasks that need concentration.

For businesses, remote work opens up a wider pool of talent. A company does not have to limit every hire to people who live near its office. This can help it find candidates with the right skills while giving employees more freedom to choose where they live.

It can also encourage better communication habits. When colleagues cannot rely on overhearing a conversation or stopping by someone’s desk, they need to share updates clearly, document decisions, and make meetings count. Those habits can help the whole team, including people who work in an office.

Remote work is not perfect. Some people miss face-to-face interaction, and others find it difficult to separate work from personal time. Teams can address these challenges with regular check-ins, clear working hours, and opportunities to connect.

The best reason to support remote work is simple: it gives people and businesses more choice. With thoughtful planning, teams can stay connected and productive while employees gain greater control over how they work.

## 2. LinkedIn post: Learning prompt engineering

### User prompt

> LinkedIn Post: Write a LinkedIn post about learning prompt engineering.

### Assistant response

I’ve been learning prompt engineering, and one thing has become clear: getting a useful AI response is less about finding a “magic prompt” and more about explaining the task well.

A small change in how I ask can make a big difference. I’ve started including the context, the outcome I want, and any constraints—then reviewing the response and refining my request.

For example, instead of asking, “Write an email,” I might ask: “Draft a concise, professional email to a client explaining an ERP reporting issue and the next steps. Keep it clear and reassuring.”

That process feels familiar from functional consulting: understand the requirement, communicate it clearly, test the result, and improve it.

I’m still learning, but that’s what makes it interesting. What’s one prompt-writing habit that has improved your AI results?

## 5. YouTube script: How to start a business

### User prompt

> YouTube Script: Write a YouTube video script about how to start a business.

### Assistant response

# YouTube Script: How to Start a Business

**[Opening — on camera]**

Want to start a business but feel like you need a perfect idea, a big budget, and a detailed plan before you begin? You don’t have to figure everything out at once.

In this video, I’ll walk you through a practical way to go from an idea to your first customer.

**[Step 1: Start with a problem]**

Instead of asking, “What business should I start?” ask, “What problem do people already want solved?”

Think about a group you understand. What takes too much of their time? What costs them money? What do they keep doing manually?

For example, if small contractors struggle to track project costs, you might explore a simple reporting service or tool. That’s a starting idea—not proof that people will buy it.

**[Step 2: Talk to potential customers]**

Before building anything, speak with people who have that problem. Ask how they handle it today, what frustrates them, and what they’ve tried. Look at existing alternatives, too. Market research helps you understand customers and find a way to stand out. [web:5]

**[Step 3: Test a small offer]**

Don’t build the full product first. Describe one clear outcome you can deliver, then test a small version of it. That could be a one-time service, a basic prototype, or a pilot with an early customer. Use their feedback to improve the offer. [web:8][web:9]

**[Step 4: Work out the numbers]**

Write down your expected startup costs, ongoing costs, price, and how you’ll reach customers. Keep the plan simple, but make sure you understand what it will take to deliver the product or service. [web:6]

**[Step 5: Set up and launch]**

Once you’re ready to operate, check the registration, tax, license, and banking requirements where you live. These depend on your location and type of business. Then put your offer in front of potential customers, listen carefully, and keep improving it. [web:5]

**[Closing — on camera]**

You don’t need to start big. Start with a real problem, test one useful solution, and learn from the people you want to serve.

What business idea are you thinking about? Share it in the comments—and tell me the problem it would solve.
